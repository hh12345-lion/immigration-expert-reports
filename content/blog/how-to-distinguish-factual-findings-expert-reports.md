Immigration expert reports may contain factual information, analysis of country conditions, and opinions based on specialist knowledge. Understanding the distinction between these elements can help legal representatives assess what a report describes, what it explains, and where an expert is expressing a professional opinion.

In UK immigration and asylum proceedings, an expert may be asked to address matters such as country conditions, the treatment of particular groups, regional circumstances, language, identity, or the significance of relevant country information. Depending on the instruction, a report may draw on published sources, documents supplied for consideration, specialist knowledge, and analysis of the available material.

These elements serve different purposes. A statement describing a published source is not necessarily the same as an independently established fact. Similarly, an expert's interpretation of country information should not be confused with a factual finding made by a tribunal.

For solicitors and barristers reviewing immigration expert evidence, it is useful to identify which statements describe information, which draw conclusions from the evidence, and which express expert opinion.

## What Is the Difference Between Factual Information and Expert Opinion?

The terms factual information, expert opinion, and factual finding have distinct meanings in the context of evidence. Their significance depends on how they are used and the context in which a statement appears.

### Factual information

Factual information describes an event, circumstance, document, observation, or other matter presented as fact.

In an immigration expert report, examples may include:

- The publication date of a country report.
- The wording of a relevant law or policy, as reproduced from an identified source.
- A documented event reported by a recognised organisation.
- Information about a country's administrative or geographical structure.
- A description of findings published by a research organisation.

However, reporting that a source makes a particular statement does not automatically establish that the underlying statement is accurate. The source, its reliability, and the context of the information may still need to be considered.

A report should make it reasonably clear whether the expert is describing what a source says or independently endorsing the information as accurate.

### Expert opinion

Expert opinion involves the interpretation or analysis of information using relevant specialist knowledge and experience.

For example, an expert may be asked to explain what published country information indicates about the treatment of a particular group, whether conditions differ between regions, or how a documented practice may operate in a particular social or cultural context.

Such an opinion should have an identifiable basis. Depending on the subject, that basis may include published research, primary sources, relevant professional experience, established specialist knowledge, or a combination of these.

The expert should explain the reasoning that connects the available information to the conclusion reached. The opinion should not simply be presented as an established fact without indicating its evidential or analytical basis.

### Factual findings made by a tribunal

A tribunal may make findings of fact when determining an appeal. These findings are distinct from factual assertions made by a party or opinions expressed by an expert.

An expert might be asked to consider an assumed factual scenario or information supplied with the instructions. The report should make clear where its analysis depends on those assumptions.

An expert's opinion does not, by itself, establish that every factual premise supplied for the analysis is true. The tribunal remains responsible for determining the relevant issues within the proceedings.

## Why the Distinction Matters in Immigration Expert Evidence

A report may draw on several types of material, including country information, documentary evidence, published research, and the expert's specialist analysis. If these elements are not clearly distinguished, a reader may find it difficult to understand how the expert reached a particular conclusion.

Separating factual information from expert opinion can help legal representatives assess:

- What information the expert relies upon.
- Whether a statement comes from a source or from the expert's own analysis.
- Which assumptions underpin the opinion.
- How the reasoning connects the evidence to the conclusion.
- Whether limitations or uncertainties have been acknowledged.
- Which questions remain matters for the tribunal to determine.

This distinction is particularly relevant where the report addresses disputed circumstances, complex country conditions, or questions that cannot be resolved by referring to a single source.

It also helps maintain a clear boundary between the expert's specialist role and the tribunal's responsibility to evaluate the evidence as a whole.

## How to Identify Factual Statements in an Expert Report

When reviewing an immigration expert report, it may be useful to examine how factual statements are presented and supported.

### Check whether the source is identified

Where a report relies on published country information, the reader should be able to identify the source of material statements.

Useful details may include the author or issuing organisation, publication date, document title, and relevant section or page reference.

These details can help the reader locate the original material and consider whether it supports the statement being made.

A reference to a source does not necessarily establish the reliability of the information. The nature of the source and the context in which it was produced may also be relevant.

### Distinguish a source's statement from an established fact

Consider the difference between these two formulations:

- "The report published by Organisation A states that the practice occurs in the region."
- "The practice occurs throughout the region."

The first statement attributes the information to a particular source. The second makes a broader factual assertion that may require additional support.

If the expert intends to reach the broader conclusion, the report should explain the evidential basis for doing so and avoid extending a source's findings beyond what they support.

### Check whether the geographical and temporal scope is clear

A factual statement may be accurate within a particular period or location without applying more widely.

For example, information about conditions in one province may not establish that identical conditions exist throughout the country. Likewise, evidence describing a historical period may not necessarily establish present conditions.

A report should distinguish between information about past events, conditions during a relevant period, and more recent circumstances where that distinction affects the analysis.

## How to Identify Expert Analysis and Opinion

Expert opinion may be expressed in different ways, depending on the subject and the questions in the instructions. The important consideration is whether the report makes clear when it moves from describing information to interpreting it.

### Look for an explanation of the reasoning

An expert opinion is easier to evaluate when the report explains how the expert reached the conclusion.

For example, an expert may analyse several sources concerning the treatment of a particular group. Rather than simply stating that the sources demonstrate a particular pattern, the report may explain how the sources relate to one another, where they agree, and what limitations affect the analysis.

The reasoning should be sufficiently clear for the reader to understand why the expert has reached the stated view.

### Identify the basis of the opinion

The basis of an expert opinion may include:

- Published research and country information.
- Primary-source material, where available and relevant.
- Specialist knowledge of the country or subject.
- Relevant professional or research experience.
- Comparison of different sources or findings.
- Analysis of the information supplied in the instructions.

Not every report will rely on all these elements. The appropriate basis depends on the subject, the scope of the instruction, and the material reasonably available.

Where an opinion relies substantially on specialist experience rather than a particular published source, the report may need to explain how that experience informs the conclusion.

### Consider whether the conclusion is appropriately qualified

Country conditions and social practices may vary by location, group, time, and individual circumstances. The available evidence may also be incomplete or inconsistent.

Where these limitations affect the analysis, an expert may need to qualify the opinion or explain the limits of what can reasonably be concluded.

For example, evidence that a practice has been documented in several locations may not establish that it occurs in every part of a country. The scope of the conclusion should remain consistent with the evidence and analysis supporting it.

Acknowledging uncertainty does not necessarily undermine an expert opinion. It can help the reader understand the extent and limits of the available evidence.

## When Factual Information and Expert Opinion Overlap

Factual information and expert analysis are not always entirely separate. Expert opinion frequently depends on factual material, while specialist analysis may help explain the significance of that material.

The distinction lies in the function of each statement and how it is supported.

### Interpreting country information

A source may document arrests, restrictions, reported incidents, or treatment affecting a particular group. An expert may then be asked to analyse whether the available material indicates a broader pattern.

The source provides information about what has been reported or documented. The expert's analysis concerns the significance of that information, including any limitations that affect the conclusion.

The report should avoid presenting an interpretation as though it were a direct quotation or an independently established fact.

### Explaining social or cultural context

An expert may be asked to explain the significance of a social practice, family relationship, community structure, or cultural expectation.

Some parts of the report may describe documented practices. Other parts may explain how those practices are understood in a particular context or how they may affect a specified issue.

The expert should make clear which information comes from identified sources and which conclusions reflect specialist interpretation. General observations should also be distinguished from claims about how every individual or community behaves.

### Applying specialist knowledge to assumed facts

Instructions may ask an expert to analyse a hypothetical or assumed factual scenario. This can be necessary where the expert's task is to explain the country-related implications if specified circumstances are accepted.

In such cases, the report should identify the assumptions on which the analysis depends.

The expert can explain the implications of those assumptions without suggesting that the underlying events have necessarily been established. The assessment of disputed facts remains a matter for the tribunal.

## Common Problems When the Distinction Is Unclear

A report may be harder to evaluate if it does not adequately distinguish between the information relied upon and the expert's conclusions.

### Conclusions are stated without sufficient reasoning

A report may express a clear opinion but provide little explanation of how the evidence supports it.

Where this occurs, the reader may have difficulty understanding the analytical steps behind the conclusion.

The issue is not simply whether the expert has expressed an opinion. It is whether the report explains the relevant reasoning and evidential basis.

### Sources are treated as conclusive without evaluation

A report may rely on a published source without addressing relevant limitations, conflicting material, or the scope of the source's findings.

A source can be relevant without resolving every question. Depending on the issue, it may be necessary to consider its methodology, date, geographical coverage, and relationship to other available evidence.

### The opinion extends beyond the material considered

A conclusion may be expressed more broadly than the sources or analysis support.

For example, evidence concerning a specific location or group may not justify a general conclusion about an entire country or population.

The scope of the opinion should reflect the material considered and the expert's relevant area of expertise.

### Assumptions are not clearly identified

If an opinion depends on facts supplied by the instructing party, a reader should be able to identify those assumptions.

Where the report does not distinguish assumed facts from independently supported information, it may become difficult to assess the precise basis of the analysis.

Clear identification of assumptions can help prevent an expert opinion from being mistaken for a finding that a disputed event occurred.

## A Practical Review Framework for Solicitors

When reviewing an immigration expert report, legal representatives may find it useful to consider the following questions.

### 1. What is the statement doing?

Determine whether the statement:

- Describes a source or document.
- Reports an event or circumstance.
- Summarises research or published findings.
- Expresses an expert interpretation.
- Reaches a conclusion based on several sources.
- Depends on facts assumed for the purpose of the analysis.

A statement may serve more than one function, but its basis should remain understandable.

### 2. Is the evidential basis identifiable?

Consider whether the report provides enough information to understand where material factual statements originate and what information supports the expert's opinion.

This may include source references, relevant dates, explanations of specialist methodology, and identification of assumptions.

### 3. Does the reasoning support the conclusion?

Consider whether the report explains the relationship between the available evidence and the opinion expressed.

A reader should be able to follow the main analytical steps without having to infer why the expert reached the conclusion.

### 4. Are limitations acknowledged?

Check whether relevant gaps, conflicting information, geographical differences, or uncertainties have been recognised where they affect the analysis.

A report need not resolve every uncertainty. However, material limitations should not be obscured by presenting a qualified conclusion as an established fact.

### 5. Does the report remain within the expert's role?

Consider whether the opinion addresses matters within the expert's relevant specialist knowledge and the scope of the instruction.

An expert may explain the significance of country information or provide specialist analysis of an assumed scenario. The expert should not present the tribunal's assessment of disputed facts or the ultimate legal determination as their own decision.

## The Importance of Clear and Transparent Reporting

Distinguishing factual information from expert opinion is part of making the reasoning in a report understandable.

A clearly structured report can identify the sources relied upon, explain relevant assumptions, distinguish description from analysis, and show how the evidence informs the expert's conclusions.

This approach may also help legal representatives identify areas requiring clarification before the report is relied upon in proceedings.

The precise requirements applicable to an expert report depend on the relevant proceedings, directions, and applicable guidance. General principles about clarity and transparency should not be treated as a substitute for checking the requirements relevant to the individual case.

## Conclusion

Factual information, source-based reporting, expert analysis, and tribunal findings serve different purposes in immigration proceedings. Although they may be connected, they should not be treated as interchangeable.

When reviewing an immigration expert report, legal representatives may wish to consider where material statements originate, how the expert has interpreted the available information, which assumptions underpin the analysis, and whether the conclusions remain within the scope of the evidence and the expert's specialist role.

A clear distinction between factual material and expert opinion can make the report's reasoning easier to assess and help the reader understand both its contribution and its limitations.

The central consideration is not simply whether a report contains facts or opinions. It is whether the basis of each material statement is sufficiently clear for its relevance and significance to be evaluated in the context of the appeal.
